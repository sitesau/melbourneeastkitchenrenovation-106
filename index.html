Treating a makeover like a full rebuild

A contained update can be a sensible project, but it is not the same risk profile as demolition, rough-in, custom joinery, stone templating, plumbing, electrical, splashback and finishing. The published scope points to design, build and installation across full rebuilds, cabinetry, joinery, stone and benchtops, makeovers and period work. That spread is the clue: the room may need one trade, several trades, or a coordinated build.

The mistake is forcing a small job onto a big-job process, or a big job onto a cosmetic budget. Ask which of these are truly changing: footprint, cabinets, services, surfaces, lighting, appliances, ventilation and compliance items. If the answer is everything, price it as a coordinated project. If the answer is surfaces only, say so plainly.

Ignoring lead time and trade order

The stated sequence is measure-up, design and quote, build and install, then handover. Cabinetry and stone fabrication sit at roughly four to six weeks, before the usually shorter on-site installation block for plumbing, electrical, tiling and finishing. Homeowners who book everything for one week often blame trades when the real issue was sequencing.

A practical habit is to map milestones backward from the date the kitchen must be usable. Leave room for templating after cabinets are set, drying or curing time, appliance delivery and final inspection items. Do not promise a family dinner deadline before stone and splashback steps are understood.

Contract and variation traps

Victoria is described as requiring a written contract for a major domestic building contract over $10,000 under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995. Domestic building insurance is described as required above the current threshold, commonly cited at $16,000, with the live figure to be checked through the relevant regulator. The mistake is treating paperwork as an afterthought.

Put scope, price, dates, payment stages, warranty terms and the variation process in writing. Hidden issues can appear after strip-out, so ask how those discoveries are documented, priced and approved before work continues. A fixed-scope quote is useful only when change is controlled in the same disciplined way.

Benchtop decisions after the silica ban

From 1 July 2024, engineered stone with 1% or more crystalline silica is described as banned for manufacture, supply, processing and installation across Australia under model WHS arrangements. That changes the showroom conversation. Low-silica options named in the source include porcelain and sintered stone, natural granite or marble, solid timber, stainless steel, laminate, or engineered stone under the 1% threshold.

Choose by use, not only by colour. Consider heat, staining, chopping habits, edge profile, seam visibility, sink and tap detail, weight, cleaning and repair. If a household wants stone looks with busy family traffic, ask how the selected material is fabricated, joined and maintained locally.

  1. Write the non-negotiables. Record layout, storage pain points, appliance positions, lighting needs and period features before pricing.
  2. Separate quote lines. Ask for cabinetry, benchtops, splashback, plumbing, electrical, appliances, finishing and paperwork to be split where practical.
  3. Confirm timing logic. Allow for measure-up, design, fabrication and on-site work rather than one compressed start date.
  4. Lock the paperwork loop. Check contract threshold, insurance wording, payment stages and the written variation process before work begins.

Common questions

What is the biggest mistake before a kitchen renovation starts? Starting with a price instead of scope. Layout, services, cabinetry, benchtop material and compliance paperwork change the job size, so a vague brief produces vague quotes.

Why does benchtop choice need extra care now? Engineered stone with 1% or more crystalline silica has been banned since 1 July 2024. The named alternatives include porcelain, sintered stone, natural stone, timber, stainless steel, laminate and low-silica engineered stone.

What should be in writing before work starts? Scope, price, dates, payment stages, warranty, compliance paperwork and a clear process for variations if hidden issues appear after strip-out.
